How molecules shape

Electron pairs around a central atom push apart. That push sets the three-dimensional shape of the molecule.

Why pairs push

Chemists call this idea VSEPR: valence shell electron pair repulsion. The outer electrons around a central atom arrange themselves so that each pair of electrons is as far from the others as it can get.

A bonded pair is shared with another atom. A lone pair stays on the central atom alone. Lone pairs take a little more room than bonded pairs, so they squeeze the bond angles down.

Count the electron domains around the central atom — each bonded group plus each lone pair — and you can predict the shape before you look at the model.

Count domainsEach lone pair counts as one. Each bonded atom counts as one, even if the bond is double or triple.
Lone pairs push harderThey sit closer to the central atom, so they bend bonded pairs away.
Shape is the atom frameWe name the shape by where the atoms sit, not by where the lone pairs sit.

03 · Quick reference

Shape at a glance

2 domains · 0 lone

Linear

CO₂. Two double bonds on opposite sides of carbon. The molecule is a straight line.

∠ O–C–O = 180°

3 domains · 0 lone

Trigonal planar

SO₃. Three oxygens around sulfur in one flat plane, 120° apart.

∠ O–S–O = 120°

4 domains · 0 lone

Tetrahedral

CH₄. Four hydrogens at the corners of a tetrahedron. No lone pairs to squeeze the angle.

∠ H–C–H ≈ 109.5°

4 domains · 1 lone

Trigonal pyramidal

NH₃. Three hydrogens plus one lone pair. The lone pair pushes the hydrogens down into a pyramid.

∠ H–N–H ≈ 107°

4 domains · 2 lone

Bent

H₂O. Two hydrogens plus two lone pairs. The lone pairs bend the molecule like a boomerang.

∠ H–O–H ≈ 104.5°

For the classroom

Check yourself

Why is CO₂ linear but H₂O bent?

Carbon in CO₂ has two electron domains and no lone pairs, so the oxygens sit opposite each other at 180°. Oxygen in H₂O has four domains — two bonded pairs and two lone pairs — so the hydrogens are pushed into a bent shape near 104.5°.

Methane and ammonia both have four electron domains. Why are their shapes different?

Methane has four bonded pairs and no lone pairs, so the atoms form a tetrahedron. Ammonia has three bonded pairs and one lone pair. We name the shape by the atoms only, so ammonia is trigonal pyramidal, and the lone pair squeezes the H–N–H angle down to about 107°.

Does a double bond count as one domain or two?

One. In VSEPR counting, a double or triple bond to the same atom is still one electron domain. That is why CO₂ has two domains, not four.

Which molecule has the smallest bond angle here, and why?

Water, at about 104.5°. It has two lone pairs. Lone pairs take more room than bonded pairs, so two of them squeeze the H–O–H angle more than ammonia’s single lone pair squeezes H–N–H.

SO₃ is trigonal planar. What would change if sulfur also kept a lone pair?

Four domains with one lone pair would make a trigonal pyramidal atom frame, like ammonia. Sulfur in SO₃ has no lone pair in the VSEPR count used here, so the three oxygens stay flat at 120°.

Where the numbers come from

  • 3D shapes of CO₂, SO₃, CH₄, NH₃ and H₂O: PubChem 3D conformers (CID 280, 24682, 297, 222, 962). BF₃, PCl₅ and SF₆ have no PubChem 3D conformer, so they are not modelled here.
  • Measured bond lengths and angles: NIST Computational Chemistry Comparison and Benchmark Database (CCCBDB), experimental data — CO₂ 180°, SO₃ 120°, CH₄ 109.471°, NH₃ 106.67°, H₂O 104.4776°.
  • VSEPR shapes follow the standard domain-counting rules taught in intro chemistry.
