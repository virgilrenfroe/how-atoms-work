Intro chemistry · Chemical bonds

How atoms bond

Atoms hold together by moving or sharing their outer electrons. There are three main ways to do it.

  1. 01Ionic: one atom gives, one takes
  2. 02Covalent: atoms share pairs
  3. 03Polar covalent: unequal sharing
  4. 04In the real world

Why bond at all?

Electrons sit in shells around the nucleus. The outer shell is the one that matters for bonding.

Atoms with a full outer shell are very stable. Neon (2, 8) and argon (2, 8, 8) already have one, so they almost never bond. For the atoms in this lesson, a full outer shell means 8 electrons, or 2 for hydrogen.

Most other atoms can reach a lower-energy, more stable arrangement by bonding. They do it in one of two ways: one atom transfers electrons to another, or two atoms share electrons.

01

Ionic bond · Na + Cl

One atom gives, one takes

Sodium has one electron in its outer shell. Chlorine has seven, one short of eight. When they meet, sodium’s outer electron moves to chlorine.

Pulling an electron off sodium costs energy, and chlorine gives back only part of it when it takes the electron. The rest comes from the ions packing into a crystal: opposite charges pull hard on each other. That pull is the ionic bond.

proton neutron electron Na⁺ Cl⁻

Shells are drawn as flat rings. Real electrons do not move in neat circles.

02

Covalent bond · shared pairs

Atoms share pairs

When neither atom can pull an electron away from the other, they share. Each shared pair is one bond. The pair counts toward the outer shell of both atoms at once.

Teal dots show only the shared electrons. Each pair circles its bond. Unshared pairs are not drawn.

03

Polar covalent bond · H₂O

Sharing, but not equally

Electronegativity is how strongly an atom pulls on shared electrons. When two different atoms share, the stronger puller holds the pair closer.

In water, oxygen (3.44) pulls harder than hydrogen (2.20). The shared pairs sit closer to oxygen. Oxygen gets a small negative charge, δ−, and each hydrogen gets a small positive charge, δ+.

Water is bent, so both δ+ ends are on one side. The whole molecule has a positive side and a negative side. Chemists call it polar.

For the classroom

Check yourself

Why does sodium become Na⁺ and not Na²⁺?

Losing one electron leaves sodium with 2, 8, a full outer shell like neon. Taking a second electron would mean breaking into that full shell, which costs far more energy.

Chloride has 18 electrons. How many protons does it have?

Still 17. Gaining an electron changes the charge, not the element. 17 protons and 18 electrons give a charge of −1.

How many shared pairs hold N₂ together?

Three. That is a triple bond, N≡N. Each nitrogen also keeps one unshared pair, so each counts 8 outer electrons.

Which bond is more polar, C–H or O–H?

O–H. The electronegativity difference is 3.44 − 2.20 = 1.24, compared with 2.55 − 2.20 = 0.35 for C–H.

Methane has four C–H bonds. Why is the molecule not polar?

C–H bonds are only slightly uneven, and the four of them point to the corners of a tetrahedron. Their small pulls cancel out, so methane has no positive side and negative side.
